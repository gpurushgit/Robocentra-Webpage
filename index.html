Why robots need vacuum-tight security

When there is a breach, something moves.

A breached laptop leaks data. A breached robot can also act in the physical world: it can change a speed limit, open a safety zone, drive into an aisle or stop a production line. Researchers have even shown that an attacker can degrade an industrial robot's accuracy so that defective parts are made without anyone noticing (Trend Micro research).

Plants and warehouses that buy their first cobot or AMR usually have an IT security team, but robots sit outside the tools and scans that team already runs. Robocentra works in that gap. We review how the robot, its controller, its fleet software and the plant network around it are set up, and we report what an attacker could do in terms both IT and operations can act on.

What vacuum-tight means

One gap is enough

Vacuum-tight means no gaps at all. For a robot that is the right standard, because an attacker only has to find one way in.

The damage is physical
A breached robot can hurt people, damage equipment and spoil product, not just expose data.
Attackers need only one way in
One weak password, one open port or one unpatched flaw is enough, while defenders have to close every one.
Robots connect everything
They sit between office IT, vendors, cloud and AI services and the floor, so a gap anywhere can lead to them.
How robots get breached

Breaches come from many directions

Stolen or default passwords
Shared or factory-set logins on robots, teach pendants and remote-support tools let anyone with the password in.
Unpatched software flaws
Known vulnerabilities in robot controllers and operating systems stay open until someone applies the vendor fix.
Internet-exposed devices
Robots, industrial routers and remote-support boxes reachable from the internet are found by automated scans.
Vendor and supplier access
Integrators and robot makers often keep remote access to your site, and a breach at their end becomes your problem.
Wireless and nearby access
Bluetooth, Wi-Fi setup and other short-range interfaces can give an attacker control without touching your network.
AI and fleet software
Cloud consoles, fleet managers and AI services that command the robots add new paths in.
Most common

But most breaches start in your IT network

In industrial settings, the attacker usually gets in through ordinary IT, then moves into the networks the robots sit on. These are the weak points we look at first.

Identity and access management (IAM)
Weak, shared or stolen accounts and too many admin rights give attackers valid logins. One over-privileged account can reach robot tools and consoles.
Phishing and email
One employee who opens the wrong message can hand an attacker a first foothold inside the company.
Remote access
VPNs, remote desktop and vendor portals that are exposed or weakly protected are a common way in.
Unpatched IT systems
Firewalls, VPN appliances and servers with known flaws are scanned for and exploited, then used as a stepping stone.
Flat networks
When office IT and robot networks are not separated, an attacker can move from a laptop to a robot controller.
Engineering workstations
The laptops used to program and maintain robots can carry malware or saved credentials into the robot network.
Ransomware
It usually arrives through the routes above and can force a precautionary shutdown of operations, even when the robots themselves are untouched.

Real robot vulnerabilities and breaches, and what they mean for a plant

Robocentra summarizes public disclosures from researchers, vendors and CISA. Each item links to the original source.

2026
Cobot controller

Universal Robots PolyScope 5 command injection

A critical flaw (CVE-2026-8153, CVSS 9.8) in the Dashboard Server lets an unauthenticated attacker who can reach its network port run commands on the robot's operating system. CISA and the vendor published advisories, and PolyScope 5.25.1 contains the fix.

For plants: Check that no cobot dashboard port is reachable from outside its cell, then schedule the update.

Read at SecurityWeek
Aug 27, 2026
Humanoid robot

Unitree G1 EDU: two paths to root

A researcher chained two flaws (CVE-2026-76639 and CVE-2026-76640) across Bluetooth, the vendor cloud, the mobile app and robot firmware to get unauthenticated root on a G1 within Bluetooth range. He showed a compromised robot could attack others nearby. The vendor patched the cloud ownership check.

For plants: Ask the vendor which firmware fixes the on-robot flaws before putting these units near production networks.

Read at Security Affairs
May 11, 2026
Fleet design

Yarbo yard robots shared one root password

A researcher found that thousands of robots used the same hardcoded root password, left remote tunnels open and protected their MQTT messaging weakly, so one robot's access reached the whole fleet. The vendor promised unique per-device credentials and a security contact.

For plants: Ask every robot vendor whether credentials are unique per device and how remote support access is controlled.

Read at Malwarebytes
Apr 2026
Robot AI software

LeRobot policy server allows remote code execution

Hugging Face's open-source robotics platform had an unsafe deserialization flaw (CVE-2026-25874, CVSS 9.3). Anyone who could reach the policy server port could send a crafted payload over gRPC and run commands on the host.

For plants: Treat AI inference servers that drive robots as part of the robot's attack surface, and keep them off flat networks.

Read at The Hacker News
2026
Research

VicOne reports 28 zero-days in robotics software

VicOne researchers reported 28 findings they classify as zero-days and demonstrated one that stopped a robot's remote-stop command from working. The count may change after vendor review.

For plants: Test that safety functions still work when the software around them is under attack.

Read at IEEE Spectrum
